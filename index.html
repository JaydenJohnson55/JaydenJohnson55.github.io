AI Coaching Chatbot

Summer 2026Scottsdale Basketball10 testsBuilt with AI help

Players at Scottsdale Basketball had questions at all hours: how to fix a jumper, what to do on rest days, how to stop spiraling after a bad game. The head coach couldn't answer them all, especially at midnight. The answer was a chatbot, built with AI's help, that responds the way he would.

What's here is a cleaned-up rebuild that can be shared publicly. The coach profile is a generic example, not the real coach.

How it works

  1. The coach's style lives in a settings file, not the code. coach_profile.json holds how he talks (short sentences, court language), what he believes (defense and effort first, fundamentals before moves), a few catchphrases, and hard rules. A coach can change their own style without touching Python.
  2. It answers from his real drills. There's a playbook of 16 drills, each tagged with words players use. For each question, the bot finds the two best matches and puts them in the prompt, so it recommends his drills instead of making new ones up.
  3. Injury questions never reach the model. If a message mentions pain, a sprain, swelling, and so on, the bot skips the AI completely and tells the player to stop and get checked by a doctor or athletic trainer.
  4. It remembers the conversation. The last 10 back-and-forths are kept so follow-ups like "what about for beginners?" make sense.
# coachbot/bot.py: safety check happens before anything else
if _INJURY.search(message):
    session.add("user", message)
    session.add("assistant", INJURY_REPLY)
    return INJURY_REPLY        # no API call, no guessing

drills = self.playbook.search(message)
system = build_system_prompt(self.profile, Playbook.format(drills))

What broke, and what I changed

How well did the drill matching actually work? The test: 20 questions written the way players really talk, each with one right drill. The first version got just 12 of 20. A question like "I get down on myself after I turn it over" slipped right past, because the drill was tagged "mistakes," and no player says "mistakes."

A synonym list fixed most of that by translating player language into the coach's terms: "turn it over" becomes mistakes, "gassed" becomes conditioning, "sore" becomes recovery. New score: 19 of 20.

That number was too good to trust. The synonyms had been written while looking at those exact 20 questions, so the test was tilted in the bot's favor. Twelve brand-new questions, never used for tuning, told the real story: 7 of 11 drills right on the first try, 9 of 11 in the top two. Keyword matching has a ceiling, and now there's a number that shows exactly where it is.

VersionRight drill firstIn top twoInjuries caught

No false injury alarms on any of the 31 drill questions.

What I'd do next

  • Swap keyword matching for embeddings, and use the held-out questions to prove it's actually better.
  • Let the coach review flagged conversations and turn good questions into new drills.
  • Save conversations to a database instead of memory.

Python, Flask, Claude API, prompt design, retrieval, pytest

Vision Production Inspection

2026AI vision project8 testsBuilt with AI help

Checking parts by eye is slow, and tired eyes miss things. This project grew out of my AI vision work on speeding up production, and it starts with a simple question: how far can plain computer vision go before you need a big AI model?

The demo inspects metal washers on a tray. Every part gets counted, and three defects get rejected: a missing center hole, an undersized part, and a chipped edge.

How it works

  1. Turn the photo gray, blur it slightly, and split bright metal from the dark tray with an automatic threshold (Otsu's method).
  2. Trace the outline of every part, plus any hole inside it.
  3. Measure each part: its size, how round it is, and whether it has a hole.
  4. Compare each part to the typical part on that tray. Because sizes are relative, moving the camera closer or farther doesn't break anything.

What broke, and what I changed

The first version scored 100% on every test. That was the red flag: the sample photos were too easy. Harder ones came next, with dim parts, glare on the tray, more camera noise, and chips as small as a fifth of the radius.

That exposed the real problem. Chips were being found by comparing each part's area to its "shrink-wrapped" outline, and a small chip barely changes that. Only 14 of 39 chipped parts got caught.

The fix: measure the distance from each part's center to every point on its edge. A round washer gives the same distance all the way around, and a chip shows up as a dip. All 39 caught.

# inspector/detect.py: a chip shows up as a dip in the edge
dists = np.hypot(pts[:, 0] - mx, pts[:, 1] - my)
edge_ratio = dists.min() / np.median(dists)   # ~0.98 round, ~0.65-0.83 chipped

Picking the cutoff meant sweeping it from 0.80 to 0.99 and charting chips caught against good parts wrongly rejected. Every good part scored 0.982 or higher; every chip scored 0.826 or lower. Anything in between works, so the setting sits at 0.90, right in the middle, leaving room for messier real photos.

Then a test caught a bug nobody would think to look for: a photo of an empty tray came back as one giant "part" with no hole. The automatic threshold always picks a cutoff, even when there's nothing there. Now the pipeline checks that parts are clearly brighter than the tray before trusting it.

The limits, plainly: the photos are computer-generated so accuracy can be measured exactly, and the tests also run on 15 fresh photos the detector never saw during tuning. Real factory photos would need the cutoffs re-tuned, and parts touching each other would need more work.

There's also an optional AI second opinion. Only rejected parts get cropped and sent to Claude's vision model to confirm, so the expensive model looks at a handful of parts instead of hundreds.

What I'd do next

  • Re-tune on real labeled photos from a production line.
  • Separate parts that touch each other.
  • Run it on a live camera feed and track reject rates by shift.

Python, OpenCV, NumPy, matplotlib, Claude vision, pytest

CRM to ERP Sync

2026Systems automation17 testsBuilt with AI help

Sales closes deals in a CRM. Finance needs those deals in the ERP to send invoices. At plenty of small businesses, someone copies them over by hand, slowly, with typos. This pipeline does it automatically.

Moving data is the easy part. The hard part is that real exports are a mess. In a sample export of 60 deals (fake companies and people), one company shows up as "Summit Fitness," "SUMMIT FITNESS," and " Summit Fitness LLC." Amounts look like $36,000.00, 36000, and 36k. Dates come in four formats.

How it works

  1. Map the columns. Known names like "Deal Owner" map instantly. When a column gets renamed, Claude suggests what it is from the name and a few sample values, and the code only accepts a suggestion that points to a real, unused field.
  2. Clean every value. Money is stored in cents, dates in one format, phone numbers as (480) 555-0101, states as two letters, and company names without "Inc." or "LLC."
  3. Reject bad rows with a reason. "TBD" as an amount or "sometime soon" as a date gets logged with the exact problem, and the good rows still go through.
  4. Load each deal exactly once. Customers are matched by email, then by company name. Each CRM deal ID can only ever create one order, so running the same file twice adds nothing.

What broke, and what I changed

What happens when someone renames six columns ("Company" to "Organization," "Close Date" to "Won On")? The first fallback, which spell-matched column names, mapped almost nothing, and every row got rejected. A second check now looks at the values themselves: a column full of email addresses is the email column, whatever it's called. After that, the renamed file mapped correctly and created zero duplicate orders.

A smaller catch: the first revenue chart skipped July because no deals closed that month, which made June look like it ran straight into August. Empty months now show up as zero.

What I'd do next

  • Connect to real systems (like HubSpot and NetSuite) instead of files.
  • Run it on a schedule and email the sales team the rejected deals to fix.
  • Have a person approve an AI column mapping the first time a new column shows up.

Python, SQL, SQLite, Claude API, matplotlib, pytest

Jarvis

2026Personal project12 testsBuilt with AI help

Resume v3. Resume v3 (1). Homework PDFs, game clips, installers run once and forgotten. Every Downloads folder ends up like this, and the goal was an assistant you could just tell "clean this up," the way you'd ask a person.

Jarvis runs on Claude's tool use. The model decides which tool to call, like "find duplicates" or "organize folder," and the code runs it. The interesting part was safety, because an AI that can move your files can also wreck them.

The safety rules

  • It can only see one folder. Every path gets checked, so something like ../ to escape the folder is refused.
  • It asks before moving anything. The yes/no prompt comes from the code, not the AI, so the model can't skip it. Say no, and the model is told, then asks what you want instead.
  • It never overwrites. If a file already exists, the new one becomes resume (1).pdf.
  • Everything can be undone. Every cleanup is logged, and "undo" puts every file back.

The duplicate trick

The obvious way to find duplicates is to fingerprint (hash) every file and compare. On a folder full of videos, that's slow. But two files can only match if they're the same size, so Jarvis groups by size first and only fingerprints files that share one. Most files never get hashed at all.
